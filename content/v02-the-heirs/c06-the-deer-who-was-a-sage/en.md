::aside{#a1 kind=note src=1.117}
**The hundred names.** Before going back to Pandu, the epic lists all of Dhritarashtra's sons in the order of their birth, from Duryodhana to the last, and adds that their sister [Duhshala](duhshala) was married in time to Jayadratha, the king of Sindhu. Every one of them is on the map, gathered around their father.
::

::scene{#s1 frame=pandu,i5755_kindama light=pandu,i5755_kindama}

One day [Pandu](pandu) was roaming the woods on the southern slopes of the Himalayas, rich in deer and wild beasts, when he saw a great stag, the leader of a herd, mating with a doe. He shot them both with five swift arrows fletched with gold. {#p1 1.118}

It was no deer. It was a sage, [Kindama](i5755_kindama), the son of a Rishi, who had taken the form of a deer to be with his mate. Struck in the act, he fell to the ground crying out in a man's voice, and wept. {#p2 1.118}

***

::scene{#s2 frame=pandu,i5755_kindama,i214_agastya light=i5755_kindama}

Even men ruled by lust and anger, he said, do nothing so cruel. How could a king of so virtuous a race lose his reason like this? {#p3 1.118}

Pandu defended himself. Kings hunt deer as they fight enemies, he said, openly or by stealth; that was the custom of kings. Did not the sage [Agastya](i214_agastya) himself once hunt every deer in the forest for his sacrifice? {#p4 1.118}

The deer answered that he did not blame Pandu for hunting. He blamed him for the moment he had chosen: what man of wisdom kills a creature in the act of love, which every creature cherishes? He had harmed no one. He lived on fruits and roots, and had taken a deer's form only because modesty would not let him do this among men. {#p5 1.118}

> As thou hast been cruel unto a couple of opposite sexes, death shall certainly overtake thee as soon as thou feelest the influence of sexual desire. {#q1 1.118}
> — Kindama

Because Pandu had not known he was a Brahmin, the sage said, the sin of killing a Brahmin would not be his. But his fate would be like Kindama's own: he would die in the arms of his wife, and that wife would follow him into the realm of the dead. *Thou hast brought me grief when I was happy,* he said. *So shall grief come to thee when thou art in happiness.* Then he died, and Pandu was overwhelmed with sorrow. {#p6 1.118}

***

::scene{#s3 frame=pandu,kunti,madri light=pandu,kunti,madri}

Pandu and his wives wept bitterly. He thought of his own father, Vichitravirya, who had died young, a slave to his passions, and of the sage who had given him life in his father's place. And here was he, the son of such a sage, wandering the woods killing deer. {#p7 1.119}

He decided to give up everything. He would shave his head, leave his wives and kin, and wander the earth alone as a beggar, sheltering under trees, treating praise and blame alike, harming no living thing. A man who could not father children, he said, should not go begging others for them. {#p8 1.119}

He told his wives to send word to Hastinapura that he had gone to the forest for good. But [Kunti](kunti) and [Madri](madri) would not be left behind. {#p9 1.119}

> O king, O thou of great wisdom, if thou abandonest us, we shall then this very day truly depart from this world. {#q2 1.119}
> — Kunti and Madri

So he agreed that they would live the forest life together: dressed in bark, living on fruits and roots, bathing at dawn and dusk, keeping the fire. He gave away the great jewel of his crown, his gold necklace, his bracelets and earrings and robes, and his wives' ornaments to the Brahmins. {#p10 1.119}

::scene{#s4 frame=pandu,dhritarashtra light=dhritarashtra}

His attendants carried the news to Hastinapura, weeping, *Oh, we are undone!* When [Dhritarashtra](dhritarashtra) heard that his brother had gone into the forest for ever, he wept for him, and for a long time took no pleasure in his bed or his seat or his food. {#p11 1.119}

***

::scene{#s5 frame=pandu,kunti,madri light=pandu}

Pandu and his wives went north, living on fruits and roots, across the mountains of Nagashata and Chaitraratha, over the Kalakuta and the Himalayas to Gandhamadana, past the lake of Indradyumna and the Hamsakuta peaks, until they came to the mountain of a hundred peaks, Shatashringa. There he stayed, and gave himself to austerity. {#p12 1.119}

In time he became a favourite of the Siddhas and Charanas who lived there. Some of the sages called him brother, some friend, some son. A Kshatriya by birth, he grew in austerity until he was almost a Brahmin sage. {#p13 1.120}
