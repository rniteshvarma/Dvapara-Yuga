::scene{#s1 frame=satyavati,vyasa light=satyavati,vyasa}

At that moment [Vyasa](vyasa) was far away, explaining the Vedas. He knew at once that his mother was calling him, and he came to her instantly, without anyone seeing him arrive. {#p1 1.105}

[Satyavati](satyavati) took her son in her arms and wept, for she had not seen him in so many years. Vyasa washed her with cool water and bowed to her. {#p2 1.105}

> I have come, O mother, to fulfil thy wishes. Therefore, O virtuous one, command me without delay. I shall accomplish thy desire. {#q1 1.105}
> — Vyasa

The family priest of the Bharatas received him with the rites due to a great sage, and Vyasa took his seat. Then his mother told him what she wanted. A mother has as much right over her sons as a father, she said. He was her eldest son; [Vichitravirya](vichitravirya) had been her youngest; so Vyasa was his brother on her side, as [Bhishma](bhishma) was on Shantanu's. Bhishma would neither father children nor rule, for the sake of truth. So she asked Vyasa to give children to his brother's widows, for the sake of the line, and of the people who needed a king. {#p3 1.105}

***

::scene{#s2 frame=vyasa,satyavati,ambika,ambalika light=vyasa}

Vyasa agreed, for the sake of what was right. He would give his brother sons *like unto Mitra and Varuna*, he said. But the widows must first keep a strict vow for a full year, to purify themselves. {#p4 1.105}

Satyavati could not wait a year. A kingdom without a king, she said, falls apart: the people have no protector, the rites stop, the rains fail. Let the princesses conceive at once, and Bhishma would watch over the children until they were born. {#p5 1.105}

Vyasa answered that if his brother was to have sons so soon, without the vow, then the princesses would have to bear his ugliness, his strong smell, his grim face and his ascetic's clothes. That would be their penance. If they could bear it, they would have excellent sons. {#p6 1.105}

::aside{#a1 kind=note src=1.105,1.106}
**"The princess of Kosala."** In these sections Ganguli often calls [Ambika](ambika) *the princess of Kosala*. She is still the eldest of the Kashi sisters who married Vichitravirya; the name comes from her mother's side. It is the same woman.
::

***

::scene{#s3 frame=ambika,vyasa,dhritarashtra light=ambika,dhritarashtra}

Satyavati went to [Ambika](ambika) and, with great difficulty, won her consent. That night the princess waited in her chamber, thinking of Bhishma and the other elders of the Kurus. When the sage came in by the light of the lamp, she saw his dark face, his matted copper-coloured locks, his blazing eyes and his grim beard, and she shut her eyes in fear. She did not open them once. {#p7 1.106}

When Vyasa came out, his mother was waiting. Would the princess have a fine son? {#p8 1.106}

> The son of the princess she will bring forth shall be equal in might unto ten thousand elephants. He will be an illustrious royal sage, possessed of great learning and intelligence and energy. The high-souled one shall have in his time a century of sons. But from the fault of his mother he shall be blind. {#q2 1.106}
> — Vyasa

*How can one that is blind become a monarch worthy of the Kurus?* Satyavati asked, and she begged him for another king. *So be it,* said Vyasa. In time Ambika gave birth to a blind son, [Dhritarashtra](dhritarashtra). {#p9 1.106}

***

::scene{#s4 frame=ambalika,vyasa,pandu light=ambalika,pandu}

Then Vyasa came to the second widow, [Ambalika](ambalika). When she saw him, she turned pale with fear. {#p10 1.106}

> Because thou hast been pale with fear at the sight of my grim visage, therefore, thy child shall be pale in complexion. O thou of handsome face, the name also thy child shall bear will be Pandu (the pale). {#q3 1.106}
> — Vyasa

Ambalika's son was born pale, but radiant, with every mark of good fortune. He was [Pandu](pandu), who would one day be the father of the Pandavas. {#p11 1.106}

***

::scene{#s5 frame=ambika,parishrami,vyasa,vidura light=parishrami,vidura}

Satyavati asked for one more son, and when Ambika's season came again she asked her to go to Vyasa once more. But Ambika remembered his grim face and his smell, and she would not go. Instead she dressed one of her maids, a girl as lovely as an Apsara, in her own ornaments, and sent her in her place. {#p12 1.106}

The maid was not afraid. She rose when the sage came in, greeted him, served him with respect, and sat beside him when he asked her to. Vyasa was pleased with her, and when he left he blessed her. {#p13 1.106}

> Amiable one, thou shalt no longer be a slave. Thy child also shall be greatly fortunate and virtuous, and the foremost of all intelligent men on earth! {#q4 1.106}
> — Vyasa

Her son was [Vidura](vidura): wise, free of desire and anger, and learned in the ways of governing. He was, the epic says, the god of justice himself, [Dharma](dharma), born on earth under the curse of the sage Mandavya. {#p14 1.106 1.63}

::aside{#a2 kind=note src=1.106,1.63}
**Two details the epic does not give.** It never names Vidura's mother; later tellings call her [Parishrami](parishrami), and so does our map. And the story of why Mandavya cursed Dharma, the sage impaled for a theft he did not commit, is told elsewhere in the first book; we will come to it in its place.
::

***

::scene{#s6 frame=dhritarashtra,pandu,vidura,vyasa,satyavati,bhishma light=dhritarashtra,pandu,vidura}

Vyasa told his mother how the eldest princess had deceived him, and how a son had been born to the maid. Then he vanished from her sight. {#p15 1.106}

So three sons were born, *in the field of Vichitravirya*, as the text says, to the island-born sage: Dhritarashtra, who was blind; Pandu, who was pale; and Vidura, the son of a maid, who was wise. From them the line of the Kurus would go on. {#p16 1.106}

None of the three could have it all. The eldest could not see; the wisest was the son of a maid; and the throne, as the next volume tells, would pass to the one in between. Bhishma, who had given up everything for his father, now stood guard over his father's grandsons, and the story of the Mahabharata could begin. {#p17 1.106}
