::scene{#s1 frame=satyavati,bhishma,ambika,ambalika light=satyavati,bhishma}

[Satyavati](satyavati) had lost both her sons. When the funeral rites were over she comforted her weeping daughters-in-law, and [Bhishma](bhishma) too, as best she could. Then she turned her mind to duty, and to the line of the Kurus, and she spoke to Bhishma. {#p1 1.103}

Everything now depended on him, she said: the offerings to Shantanu's ancestors, the memory of his deeds, the survival of his line. Bhishma knew the law as well as the teachers of the gods. Her son, his brother, had gone to heaven childless, and his young widows longed for children. {#p2 1.103}

> Therefore, O thou of mighty arms, at my command, raise offspring on them for the perpetuation of our line. It behoveth thee to guard virtue against loss. Install thyself on the throne and rule the kingdom of the Bharatas. Wed thou duly a wife. Plunge not thy ancestors into hell. {#q1 1.103}
> — Satyavati

***

::scene{#s2 frame=bhishma,satyavati light=bhishma}

She was asking him to undo both his vows at once: to take the throne, and to marry. Bhishma answered that what she said was right by the law. But she knew his vow, and she knew why he had made it, for it had been made for her. {#p3 1.103}

> O Satyavati, I repeat the pledge I once gave, viz., I would renounce three worlds, the empire of heaven, anything that may be greater than that, but truth I would never renounce. {#q2 1.103}
> — Bhishma

Then he went further, and named everything in the world that might give up its nature before he gave up his word. {#p4 1.103}

> The earth may renounce its scent, water may renounce its moisture, light may renounce its attribute of exhibiting forms, air may renounce its attribute of touch, the sun may renounce his glory, fire, its heat, the moon, his cooling rays, space, its capacity of generating sound, the slayer of Vritra, his prowess, the god of justice, his impartiality; but I cannot renounce truth. {#q3 1.103}
> — Bhishma

Satyavati was weeping now. She knew he could create three new worlds if he wished, she said, and she knew what he had sworn on her account. But this was an emergency. Let him bear the duty a man owes his ancestors, so that the line would not be broken. {#p5 1.103}

The epic says plainly that grief had made her speak against what was right. Bhishma would not move. *O Queen,* he said, *turn not thy eyes away from virtue. O, destroy us not.* A Kshatriya who breaks his word is never praised. But there was an old custom, he told her, that had saved royal lines before, and he would tell her of it. {#p6 1.103}

***

::scene{#s3 frame=bhishma,jamadagni,parashurama light=parashurama}

The first example he gave was a terrible one. Long ago, [Rama the son of Jamadagni](parashurama), furious at the killing of his father, had cut down the thousand-armed king of the Haihayas, and then gone on to destroy the whole Kshatriya order of the earth, twenty-one times over. The kingly line had survived only because the Kshatriya women had borne children to learned Brahmins. Those sons, the Vedas say, belonged to the women's husbands, and so the Kshatriyas lived on. {#p7 1.104}

::scene{#s4 frame=i3619_dirghatamas,i1198_bali,i10168_sudeshna light=i3619_dirghatamas,i1198_bali}

The second was the story of a blind sage. [Dirghatamas](i3619_dirghatamas), the son of [Utathya](i11234_utathya), was born blind because of a curse, and grew into a great master of the Vedas. In his old age his own wife and sons turned on him, tied him to a raft and set him adrift on the Ganga. He floated past the lands of many kings, until a good king, [Bali](i1198_bali), found him while bathing in the river and drew him out. {#p8 1.104}

When Bali learned who he was, he asked the old sage to give him sons. Dirghatamas agreed. Bali's queen, [Sudeshna](i10168_sudeshna), would not go to a blind old man, and sent her nurse instead, and the eleven sons born of that union were the sage's, not the king's. But when the king pleaded with him, Dirghatamas blessed Sudeshna herself, and she bore five sons: Anga, Vanga, Kalinga, Pundra and Suhma, whose names live on in five kingdoms. {#p9 1.104}

::aside{#a1 kind=note src=1.104}
**An old custom, carefully told.** The custom Bhishma describes, in which a husband or his family appoints another man to father children for him, is called *niyoga*. The epic treats it as lawful only in a crisis, when a line would otherwise end, and the children are counted as the husband's. Ganguli left a few lines of Bhishma's second story untranslated, in Latin, and we have summarised them only briefly here.
::

*Hearing this, O mother,* Bhishma finished, *do as thou likest.* And he suggested that a learned Brahmin be invited, with an offer of wealth, to give children to Vichitravirya's widows. {#p10 1.104 1.105}

***

::scene{#s5 frame=satyavati,bhishma,vyasa,parashara light=satyavati,vyasa}

Satyavati smiled a little, and when she spoke her voice was broken with shyness. Bhishma was right, she said, and she trusted him. So she would tell him something from her own past. {#p11 1.105}

She told him about her father's ferry, and about the sage [Parashara](parashara), the fog on the Yamuna, and the son she had borne on an island in the river while still a maiden. That son was now a great sage. He had arranged the Vedas, and so was called Vyasa; and because he was dark he was called Krishna. When he left her, he had told her to think of him when she was in trouble. {#p12 1.105}

> I will now call him up, if thou, O Bhishma of mighty arms so desirest. If thou art willing, O Bhishma, I am sure that great ascetic will beget children upon Vichitravirya's field. {#q4 1.105}
> — Satyavati

At the mention of the great sage, Bhishma joined his palms. What she proposed, he said, was both for their good and in keeping with the law, and he approved of it with all his heart. {#p13 1.105}

So Satyavati thought of her first son. {#p14 1.105}
