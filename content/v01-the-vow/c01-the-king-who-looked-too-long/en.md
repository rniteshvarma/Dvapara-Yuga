::scene{#s1 frame=brahma,ganga light=brahma,ganga}

Long before Kurukshetra, before the Pandavas and before Bhishma, there was a king named Mahabhisha. He came of the line of Ikshvaku and ruled the whole earth, truthful in his speech and true in his strength. He performed a thousand horse-sacrifices and a hundred Rajasuyas, and with them he so pleased Indra, the lord of the gods, that when his life on earth was over he rose to heaven. {#p1 1.96}

One day the gods gathered to worship [Brahma](brahma), the Grandsire of the worlds. Royal sages sat among them, and Mahabhisha was one of them. Then [Ganga](ganga), the queen of rivers, came to pay her respects. The wind caught her garment, white as moonlight, and lifted it. Every god bowed his head. Mahabhisha did not. He looked, and went on looking. {#p2 1.96}

Brahma saw it, and spoke. {#p3 1.96}

> Wretch, as thou hast forgotten thyself at the sight of Ganga, thou shalt be re-born on earth. But thou shall again and again attain to these regions. And she, too, shall be born in the world of men and shall do thee injuries. But when thy wrath shall be provoked, thou shalt then be freed from my curse. {#q1 1.96}
> — Brahma

It was a strange curse, half punishment and half promise. Mahabhisha would fall to earth, and Ganga would follow him. She would hurt him, and on the day he lost his temper with her he would be free. Mahabhisha thought over every king and every sage on earth, and chose his father in advance: he would be born as the son of [Pratipa](pratipa), a king of great prowess. {#p4 1.96}

Ganga had seen the king lose his composure. She left the assembly thinking of him. {#p5 1.96}

***

::scene{#s2 frame=ganga,prabhasa,vasishtha light=prabhasa,vasishtha}

On her way she met the eight Vasus, gods of the elements, walking the same road with downcast faces. *Why look ye so dejected?* she asked them. They told her they had been cursed by the great sage [Vasishtha](vasishtha) to be born among men. {#p6 1.96}

They had a request. They could not bear to enter a human womb, so would Ganga take a woman's form and be their mother? Their father, they said, would be a king who was not yet born: [Shantanu](shantanu), the son of Pratipa. Ganga answered that this was exactly her own wish. Then the Vasus asked for one thing more. As each of them was born, she must throw him into the water, so that his time on earth would be over almost before it began. {#p7 1.96}

Ganga agreed, but she set a condition of her own. Her life with this king must not leave him with nothing; one son at least must live. So the Vasus each gave an eighth part of their own energy, and from these eight parts one son would be made. He would live, and he would be mighty. But he would never have children of his own. {#p8 1.96}

::aside{#a1 kind=versions src=1.96,1.99}
**The epic gives two reasons for the curse.** Here the Vasus say they offended Vasishtha by passing him unseen while he sat at his evening prayers, which they call a small fault. Three sections later Ganga tells Shantanu a different and far more famous story: one of the Vasus stole the sage's wish-granting cow. The Mahabharata keeps both, and so do we, in the order it tells them.
::

***

::scene{#s3 frame=pratipa,ganga light=pratipa,ganga}

Pratipa, king of the Kurus, was kind to every living creature. For many years he practised austerities at the source of the Ganga. One day the river herself rose out of the water in the form of a lovely woman, walked up to the king and sat down on his right thigh. {#p9 1.97}

He asked her what she wanted. *I desire thee, O king, for my husband,* she said. *To refuse a woman coming of her own accord is never applauded by the wise.* {#p10 1.97}

Pratipa refused her all the same. He had taken a vow never to go to another man's wife or to a woman not of his own order. And she had chosen her seat badly, he told her: the right thigh is the place of daughters and daughters-in-law, and only the left belongs to a wife. {#p11 1.97}

> Therefore, O best of women, I cannot enjoy thee as an object of desire. Be my daughter-in-law. I accept thee for my son! {#q2 1.97}
> — Pratipa

She accepted, with one condition, and the whole of the next chapter is already folded inside it. When she became the wife of his son, his son must never judge whether what she did was right or wrong. Living like that, she promised, she would bring him happiness, and through the sons she bore him he would reach heaven. Then she vanished. {#p12 1.97}

***

::scene{#s4 frame=pratipa,sunanda,shantanu,devapi,bahlika light=shantanu}

Pratipa and his queen, [Sunanda](sunanda) of the Shibi country, practised austerities in the hope of a son. When they were old, a son was born to them, and he was none other than Mahabhisha, come down to earth as Brahma had said. They named him Shantanu, because he was born after his father had stilled his passions through penance. {#p13 1.97}

::aside{#a2 kind=note src=1.95}
**Shantanu was not an only son.** The genealogy in Section 95 gives Pratipa three sons: [Devapi](devapi), Shantanu and [Bahlika](bahlika). Devapi, the eldest, went to the forest as a hermit while still a boy, and so the throne passed to Shantanu.
::

When Shantanu was grown, his father took him aside. A heavenly woman, he said, had once come to him for Shantanu's sake. If she met him alone and asked him for children, he must take her as his wife. He must never ask who she was or where she came from, and never judge what she did. Then Pratipa set his son on the throne and went away into the forest. {#p14 1.97}

***

::scene{#s5 frame=shantanu,ganga light=shantanu,ganga}

Shantanu was a just king and a tireless hunter, and he spent much of his time in the woods. One day, walking alone along the bank of the Ganges, he came upon a woman of blazing beauty, *like unto another Sri herself*, dressed in garments as fine as the filaments of a lotus. {#p15 1.97}

He could not take his eyes from her. *With steadfast gaze he seemed to be drinking her charms,* the text says, *but repeated draughts failed to quench his thirst.* It is the same long look, we might notice, that once cost Mahabhisha his place in heaven. And this time she looked back. {#p16 1.97}

> O slender-waisted one, be thou a goddess or the daughter of a Danava, be thou of the race of the Gandharvas, or Apsaras, be thou of the Yakshas or the Nagas, or be thou of human origin, O thou of celestial beauty, I solicit thee to be my wife! {#q3 1.97}
> — Shantanu

She listened to the smiling king, and remembered her promise to the Vasus. {#p17 1.98}
