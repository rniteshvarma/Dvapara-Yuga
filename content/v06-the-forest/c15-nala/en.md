::scene{#s1 frame=bhima,yudhishthira light=bhima}

With Arjuna gone, [Bhima](bhima) grew restless again. Everything depended on Arjuna, he told [Yudhishthira](yudhishthira), and Yudhishthira had sent him away. Their misery came from his gambling. Let them call back Arjuna and Krishna and kill the Kauravas now. The Veda says a day of hard vows equals a year; count a day and a little more as thirteen years. And how could they ever hide for a year from Duryodhana's spies? {#p1 3.52}

Yudhishthira held him back once more. Wait for the thirteenth year, he said. {#p2 3.52}

***

::scene{#s2 frame=yudhishthira light=yudhishthira}

Just then the sage Brihadashva came. Yudhishthira welcomed him, sat beside him, and poured out his grief: cheated at dice, his wife dragged into the assembly, his brother gone. {#p3 3.52}

> Is there a king on this earth who is more unfortunate than myself? {#q1 3.52}
> — Yudhishthira

There is, said Brihadashva, and he told him the story of Nala. {#p4 3.52}

***

::scene{#s3 frame=yudhishthira light=yudhishthira}

Nala was king of the Nishadhas, a great horseman and a good man. A golden swan carried word of him to Damayanti, princess of Vidarbha, and she fell in love with him before they met. At her svayamvara four gods came in Nala's own shape, so she could not tell which was he. She prayed, and they showed her the signs of gods: they did not sweat or blink, their garlands did not fade, their feet did not touch the ground. She chose the one man among the five. {#p5 3.53 3.57}

But Kali, the spirit of strife, had wanted her too, and swore revenge. He waited twelve years for a flaw, and found it the day Nala said his evening prayers without first washing his feet. Kali entered him, and urged Nala's brother Pushkara to challenge him. Dvapara, Kali's companion, became the die. {#p6 3.59}

> Thus challenged in the presence of Damayanti, the lofty-minded king could not long decline it. {#q2 3.59}
> — Brihadashva

***

::scene{#s4 frame=yudhishthira light=yudhishthira}

Nala lost everything, even his clothes, and went into the forest with Damayanti, sharing one cloth between them. In the night, out of his mind, he cut the cloth in half and left her sleeping, hoping she would go home to her father. {#p7 3.61 3.62}

A serpent king, Karkotaka, bit him and changed his shape, so no one would know him. Under the name Vahuka he became charioteer to King Rituparna of Ayodhya. Damayanti, after long wandering, reached her father's house and searched for him. At last she sent word of a second svayamvara, knowing only Nala could drive a chariot fast enough to get Rituparna there in a day. {#p8 3.66 3.67 3.70}

On that drive, Nala and the king traded secrets: Nala's knowledge of horses for Rituparna's knowledge of dice. {#p9 3.72}

> As solicited by thee, receive this science of dice from me, and, O Vahuka, let my equine science remain with thee in trust. {#q3 3.72}
> — Rituparna

The moment Nala knew the science of dice, Kali came out of him, vomiting Karkotaka's poison. {#p10 3.72}

***

::scene{#s5 frame=yudhishthira light=yudhishthira}

Damayanti knew him, and they were together again. Nala went home and challenged Pushkara to play once more, and won everything back in a single throw. Then he forgave his brother. {#p11 3.76 3.78}

> Thou knowest it not, O fool, that it was Kali who did it all. I shall not, therefore, impute to thee the faults of others. {#q4 3.78}
> — Nala

*O Pushkara, thou art my brother, live thou for a hundred years!* {#p12 3.78}

***

::scene{#s6 frame=yudhishthira,arjuna light=yudhishthira}

Nala had been alone in the forest, said Brihadashva; Yudhishthira had his brothers, his wife and his Brahmanas. He had little cause for sorrow. And then he offered more. {#p13 3.79}

> I know the science of dice in its entirety. I am gratified with thee; take this lore, O son of Kunti, I will tell unto thee. {#q5 3.79}
> — Brihadashva

So Yudhishthira learned the science of dice, and the sage went away. And Yudhishthira heard from travellers that Arjuna was living on air alone in the mountains, in silence, and he grieved for him. {#p14 3.79}

::aside{#a1 kind=note src=3.52,3.79}
**The tale within the tale.** Nala and Damayanti fill twenty-seven sections of the Vana Parva, and it is one of the most loved love stories in Sanskrit. It is told here only in outline; a companion volume will tell it in full. Notice what it is doing here, though: a king ruined at dice by his own kinsman, a wife abandoned in one cloth, and the die itself possessed by Dvapara, the spirit of the very age this whole epic takes place in.
::
