With both armies drawn up, [Dhritarashtra](dhritarashtra) asked [Sanjaya](sanjaya) to tell him everything. He believed destiny was stronger than effort, he said; he could see the evil of the war, but whenever he came near Duryodhana his understanding turned away. {#p1 5.160}

Sanjaya would not let him put it all on his son. The Pandavas had borne the injuries of the dice looking only to the king's face. A man who comes to grief through his own misconduct should not blame the gods or time. {#p2 5.160}

> In this respect, three opinions are entertained; some say that everything is ordained by God; some say that our acts are the result of free-will; and others say that our acts are the result of those of our past lives. {#q1 5.160}
> — Sanjaya

***

::scene{#s2 frame=duryodhana,uluka,karna,shakuni light=duryodhana}

On the bank of the Hiranvati the Pandavas had made camp, and the Kurus made theirs. [Duryodhana](duryodhana) talked with Karna, Duhshasana and Shakuni, then called [Uluka](uluka), Shakuni's son, in private. Go to the Pandavas, he said, and in Vasudeva's hearing tell them this. {#p3 5.161}

To Yudhishthira he sent a story Narada once told his father. A wicked cat lived on the bank of the Ganga with its paws raised like an ascetic, saying *I am practising virtue*. The birds trusted it; then the mice asked it to be their uncle and protect them. The cat agreed, if they would carry it to the river every day, being so weak from its vows. It grew fat, and the mice grew fewer. {#p4 5.161}

A clever mouse named Dindika said he would walk behind with their uncle; the cat ate him. Then an old mouse, Kilika, spoke up: the droppings of a creature that lives on fruit and roots do not have fur in them, and Dindika had not been seen for eight days. The mice ran in every direction. {#p5 5.161}

> O thou of wicked soul, thou too art a practiser of such feline behaviour. Thou behavest towards thy kinsmen after the manner of the cat (in the story) towards the mice. Thy speech is of one kind, and thy conduct is of another. {#q2 5.161}
> — Duryodhana

He had begged for just five villages, and been refused, Duryodhana said, because they wanted war. Let him remember Vidura driven out and the house of lac, and be a man. {#p6 5.161}

***

::scene{#s3 frame=duryodhana,krishna,bhima,arjuna light=duryodhana}

To Krishna: put on that form again that you showed in the court; conjurer's tricks may frighten some, but they only anger an armed man. *A slave of Kansa*, Duryodhana called him, not worth putting on armour against. To Bhima: the cook Vallabha of Virata's kitchen, let him drink Duhshasana's blood if he could. To Shikhandi: Bhishma would not kill him, knowing him for a woman, so let him fight without fear. {#p7 5.161}

And to Arjuna, at length. He knew Arjuna had Vasudeva, and a Gandiva six cubits long, and that there was no one his equal; knowing it all, he kept the kingdom still. Where was Gandiva when they were won as slaves? It was Draupadi who freed them. Had Arjuna not worn a braid and taught girls to dance at Virata's court? {#p8 5.161}

> A thousand Vasudevas, a hundred Falgunis, approaching me whose arms and weapons never go for nothing, will surely fly away in all directions. {#q3 5.161}
> — Duryodhana

His army was an ocean, Kripa its great fish, Bhishma its current, Drona its alligator, Shakuni its shores. When Arjuna had plunged into it and lost his friends, he would turn from the thought of a kingdom *like the heart of a person of impure deeds turning away from (hope of) heaven*. {#p9 5.161}
