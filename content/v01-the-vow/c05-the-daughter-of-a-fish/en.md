::aside{#a1 kind=note src=1.63}
**Where this story sits.** The Mahabharata tells the birth of Satyavati and of her first son in its long first book, well before Shantanu is born. We tell it here, where she enters the story of the Kurus.
::

::scene{#s1 frame=uparichara,indra light=uparichara}

There was once a king named Vasu, of the Paurava line, who loved virtue and loved hunting almost as much. At Indra's bidding he conquered the rich kingdom of Chedi. Later he laid down his arms and turned to such fierce austerities that the gods grew uneasy, thinking he wanted to take Indra's place. They came to him with gentle words and won him back to ruling. {#p1 1.63}

[Indra](indra) made him a friend. He gave him a crystal chariot that flew through the air, so that Vasu alone of all mortals could course through the sky like a god, and a garland of unfading lotuses that kept him from harm in battle. Because he rode above the earth he was called [Uparichara](uparichara), *the one who moves on high*. He ruled from Chedi, and he had five sons, whom he set over provinces of their own. {#p2 1.63}

***

::scene{#s2 frame=uparichara,i4159_girika,adrika light=uparichara,adrika}

His queen was [Girika](i4159_girika), and he loved her deeply. One spring her season came, and on that very day the spirits of his ancestors asked him to hunt deer for their rites. Vasu would not disobey them, so he went into the forest, thinking only of Girika. {#p3 1.63}

The woods were in full flower, and he was overcome with longing for her. Sitting beneath an ashoka tree, he gave his seed to a swift hawk to carry back to his wife. {#p4 1.63}

On its way the hawk was attacked by another hawk, who thought it was carrying meat. As the two birds fought in the sky, the seed fell into the waters of the Yamuna. {#p5 1.63}

In that river lived an apsara named [Adrika](adrika), whom a Brahmin's curse had turned into a fish. She swallowed the seed. In the tenth month the fish was caught by fishermen, and when they opened her they found two human children, a boy and a girl. {#p6 1.63}

The fishermen took them to their king, Uparichara. He kept the boy, who grew up to be the truthful king [Matsya](i7223_matsya). And Adrika, who had been told that she would be freed once she bore two human children, left her fish-form and rose into the sky. {#p7 1.63}

***

::scene{#s3 frame=satyavati,dasharaja,uparichara light=satyavati}

The girl the king gave to the fishermen. *Let this one be thy daughter,* he said. Her name was [Satyavati](satyavati). She was beautiful and good, but because she had grown up among fishermen she carried the smell of fish. To help her father she rowed a ferry across the Yamuna. {#p8 1.63}

::scene{#s4 frame=satyavati,parashara light=parashara,satyavati}

One day the great sage [Parashara](parashara), wandering the land, came to her boat. He saw her and desired her at once. She was afraid: there were sages standing on both banks of the river, she told him, and they would see. So Parashara drew a fog over the whole river, so thick that no one could see anything at all. {#p9 1.63}

Still she hesitated. She was a maiden in her father's care, she said; if she lost her maidenhood she could never go home, and would not want to live. Parashara promised that she would remain a maiden, and told her to ask for any boon she wished. {#p10 1.63}

Satyavati asked that her body should smell sweet instead of the fish-smell she had always had. He granted it. From then on she was called Gandhavati, *the sweet-scented*, and Yojanagandha, because her fragrance carried for a *yojana* all around. {#p11 1.63}

***

::scene{#s5 frame=satyavati,vyasa,parashara light=vyasa}

That same day, on an island in the Yamuna, she gave birth to Parashara's son. With his mother's leave, the child turned at once to the life of an ascetic. Before he went, he made her a promise. {#p12 1.63}

> As soon as thou rememberest me when occasion comes, I shall appear unto thee. {#q1 1.63}
> — Vyasa

Because he was born on an island, a *dvipa*, he was called Dvaipayana. Later he divided and arranged the Vedas, and for that he was called [Vyasa](vyasa), *the arranger*. He taught the Vedas to his students, and the Mahabharata with them as a fifth: to Sumantu, Jaimini and Paila, to his own son [Shuka](shuka), and to [Vaishampayana](vaishampayana). {#p13 1.63}

::aside{#a2 kind=note src=1.63}
**Who is telling this story?** Almost every section of the epic begins *Vaisampayana said*. The Mahabharata we are reading is told by Vaishampayana, the student of Vyasa, who is himself the son of the girl on the ferry. So the author of the epic is also one of its characters, and he will step into the story at the moment his mother remembers him.
::

Satyavati's maidenhood remained whole, as the sage had promised, and her sweet scent went with her wherever she went. It was that scent which, years later, led King Shantanu through the woods to her boat. {#p14 1.63 1.100}
