::scene{#s1 frame=draupadi,yudhishthira light=draupadi}

One evening, sitting together in their grief, [Draupadi](draupadi) spoke to [Yudhishthira](yudhishthira). {#p1 3.27}

Duryodhana felt no sorrow, she said. When they left in deer-skins, only four men in Hastinapura had not wept: Duryodhana, Karna, Shakuni and Duhshasana. She looked at Yudhishthira's bed of kusha grass and remembered his ivory throne; at the mud on his body, once smeared with sandal; at his rags, once white silk. She looked at Bhima, waiting only for his brother's word; at Arjuna, who could loose five hundred arrows at one draw; at the twins; at herself, Drupada's daughter, Pandu's daughter-in-law. *Why doth not thy anger, O king, blaze up?* {#p2 3.27}

> It is said that there is no Kshatriya in the world who is bereft of anger. I now behold in thee, however, a refutation of the proverb! {#q1 3.27}
> — Draupadi

***

::scene{#s2 frame=draupadi light=draupadi}

She told him what the demon king Prahlada once taught his grandson Bali, who asked whether forgiveness or force was better. Neither always, Prahlada said. A man who always forgives is despised by his servants and robbed by strangers; a man who never forgives makes enemies of everyone. There is a time for each. {#p3 3.28}

> He, indeed, is a king who hath recourse to both, each according to its time! {#q2 3.28}
> — Draupadi

And this, she said, was not the time for forgiveness. {#p4 3.28}

***

::scene{#s3 frame=yudhishthira,draupadi light=yudhishthira}

Yudhishthira answered that anger is the slayer of men. An angry man kills his teachers, insults his elders, says what should not be said; there is nothing he will not do. And he recited to her the verses of the sage Kashyapa on forgiveness. {#p5 3.29}

> Forgiveness is the might of the mighty; forgiveness is sacrifice; forgiveness is quiet of mind. {#q3 3.29}
> — Kashyapa, quoted by Yudhishthira

Bhishma would counsel peace, he said, and Krishna, Drona, Vidura, Kripa, Sanjaya, Vyasa. Urged by them all, Dhritarashtra would surely give back the kingdom. Duryodhana could not forgive because he did not deserve the kingdom. *I, however, deserve the sovereignty and therefore is it that forgiveness hath taken possession of me.* {#p6 3.29}

***

::scene{#s4 frame=draupadi,yudhishthira light=draupadi}

Then Draupadi said something more dangerous. She bowed, she said, to the gods who had clouded his mind. He loved virtue more than his brothers, more than her, more than his life, and where had virtue brought him? {#p7 3.30}

> O hero amongst men, as a wooden doll is made to move its limbs by the wire-puller, so are creatures made to work by the Lord of all. {#q4 3.30}
> — Draupadi

God plays with his creatures, she said, as a child plays with clay toys. He seems to treat them not like a father or mother but in anger: she saw good men suffering and wicked men prosper, and she blamed the Creator for it. {#p8 3.30}

***

::scene{#s5 frame=yudhishthira,draupadi light=yudhishthira}

> Thou speakest, however, the language of atheism. {#q5 3.31}
> — Yudhishthira

He did not do good for its rewards, he told her. He gave because it was right to give and sacrificed because it was right to sacrifice. {#p9 3.31}

> The man who wisheth to reap the fruits of virtue is a trader in virtue. {#q6 3.31}
> — Yudhishthira

Do not doubt dharma or blame God, he said. Dharma is the only raft to the other shore. {#p10 3.31}

***

::scene{#s6 frame=draupadi,drupada light=draupadi}

Draupadi said she did not scorn dharma or God. But a man must act. A farmer ploughs and sows, and then the rain is up to the clouds, and if no rain comes he is not to blame. Success needs fate and chance and effort together; without effort, there is nothing. {#p11 3.32}

> If however, no exertion is made, there can be no success. {#q7 3.32}
> — Draupadi

She had learned all this as a girl, she said, from a learned Brahmana in her father's house, who taught it to her brothers. When she slipped out of the women's rooms and sat on her father's lap, he would recite it to her too. {#p12 3.32}

::aside{#a1 kind=note src=3.32}
**Draupadi the scholar.** This chapter is one of the longest arguments a woman makes anywhere in the epic: on anger, on God, on fate and effort. It quotes Brihaspati's political science, and she tells us where she learned it. Retellings that remember only her anger leave most of her out.
::
