::aside{#a1 kind=note src=1.106,1.107}
**Where we are.** Volume I ended with three boys born to the sage Vyasa: Dhritarashtra, Pandu and Vidura. It said that Vidura was the god of justice himself, born on earth under a sage's curse. King Janamejaya, to whom this whole story is being told, asks at once what the god had done. This is the answer.
::

::scene{#s1 frame=i623_animandavya,dharma light=i623_animandavya}

There was a Brahmin named [Mandavya](i623_animandavya), devoted to truth and to austerity. For years he sat at the entrance of his hermitage, at the foot of a tree, his arms raised above his head, keeping a vow of silence. {#p1 1.107}

One day a band of robbers came running into the hermitage, laden with stolen goods and chased by the king's guards. They hid their loot there and hid themselves nearby. The guards arrived a moment later, saw the sage sitting under the tree, and asked him which way the thieves had gone. {#p2 1.107}

Mandavya was keeping his vow. He said nothing, good or bad. {#p3 1.107}

The guards searched the hermitage and found the thieves and the loot, and now they suspected the silent sage too. They seized him with the robbers and brought him before the king, and the king sentenced them all to death. Acting in ignorance, the officers carried out the sentence: they impaled the sage on a stake. {#p4 1.107}

***

::scene{#s2 frame=i623_animandavya light=i623_animandavya}

Mandavya did not die. He hung on the stake, without food, for a long time, sustained by the power of his austerities, still deep in meditation. Other sages came to him at night in the form of birds, and grieved to see him, and asked him what sin he had committed to suffer like this. {#p5 1.107}

> Whom shall I blame for this? In fact, none else (than my own self) hath offended against me! {#q1 1.108}
> — Mandavya

When the king heard that the sage was still alive, he hurried to him with his counsellors and begged forgiveness: he had wronged him in ignorance. Mandavya forgave him. But when the king had him taken down, the stake could not be drawn out of his body, so the king cut it off just where it entered. With the point of the stake still inside him, Mandavya walked the earth and practised austerities so great that he won worlds no one else could reach. That is why the three worlds call him Ani-Mandavya, *Mandavya with the stake within*. {#p6 1.108}

***

::scene{#s3 frame=i623_animandavya,dharma light=dharma,i623_animandavya}

One day Ani-Mandavya went to the court of the god of justice, [Dharma](dharma), and found him on his throne. *What, pray, is that sinful act committed by me unconsciously, for which I am bearing this punishment?* he demanded. *O, tell me soon, and behold the power of my asceticism.* {#p7 1.108}

Dharma told him. When he was a child, Mandavya had once pierced a little insect on a blade of grass. Even a small gift multiplies its merit, the god said, and in the same way even a small sin multiplies the suffering it brings. {#p8 1.108}

Mandavya asked when he had done this, and Dharma said: when he was a child. Then the sage laid down a new law. What a child does in its earliest years is no sin, he said; the punishment had been out of all proportion to the fault; and the killing of a Brahmin is a heavier sin than the killing of any other living being. {#p9 1.108}

> Thou shall, therefore, O god of justice, have to be born among men even in the Sudra order. {#q2 1.108}
> — Mandavya

::aside{#a2 kind=versions src=1.108}
**Twelve years or fourteen?** In the same speech Mandavya first says a child is free of sin *up to the twelfth year*, and then, laying down the rule for the future, sets the limit at *the age of fourteen*. Ganguli's translation gives both, and we leave them as the text has them.
::

So the god of justice was born on earth as [Vidura](vidura), the son of a maid: wise in law and in the ways of the world, free of greed and anger, far-sighted, calm of mind, and always devoted to the good of the Kurus. {#p10 1.108}

***

::scene{#s4 frame=bhishma,dhritarashtra,pandu,vidura light=dhritarashtra,pandu,vidura}

With the birth of the three boys, the land of the Kurus began to flourish. The harvests were rich and the rain came in its season; the trees were heavy with fruit and flowers; the cities filled with merchants and craftsmen. There were no robbers and no one wicked, and it seemed that the golden age had come back. In the houses of the Kurus, the text says, *'give', 'eat' were the only words constantly heard.* {#p11 1.109}

[Bhishma](bhishma) brought up [Dhritarashtra](dhritarashtra), [Pandu](pandu) and Vidura as if they were his own sons. They studied the Vedas and the law, history and the Puranas, and learned the bow, the horse, the mace, the sword and the war-elephant. Pandu became the finest archer of all; Dhritarashtra the strongest; and in all the three worlds there was no one equal to Vidura in virtue and in knowledge of what was right. {#p12 1.109}

People everywhere began to say that among mothers of heroes the princesses of Kashi came first; among countries, the land of the Kurus; among the virtuous, Vidura; and among cities, Hastinapura. {#p13 1.109}

::scene{#s5 frame=pandu,dhritarashtra,vidura,bhishma light=pandu}

And Pandu became king. Dhritarashtra could not, because he was blind; and Vidura could not, because his mother was a Shudra woman. {#p14 1.109}
