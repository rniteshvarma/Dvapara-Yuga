::scene{#s1 frame=pandu,kunti,madri light=pandu}

One day, at the new moon, the great sages of the mountain set out north to see [Brahma](brahma), for the gods, the sages and the ancestors were gathering in his realm. [Pandu](pandu) rose to follow them with his wives. But the sages warned him: the high Himalayas were a place of eternal snow and endless rain, where not even birds could pass and only the wind, the Siddhas and the great Rishis could go. How could the princesses climb there? {#p1 1.120}

Pandu's answer came out of a deeper grief. {#p2 1.120}

> Ye fortunate ones, it is said that for the sonless there is no admittance into heaven. I am sonless! {#q1 1.120}
> — Pandu

Every man is born with four debts, he said: to the gods, paid by sacrifice; to the sages, by study and austerity; to other men, by a kind and harmless life; and to the ancestors, by fathering children and making offerings for them. He had paid the first three. The last he could not pay, and with his death his ancestors would be lost. Might sons be born in his field, he asked them, as he himself had been born in his father's, through a great sage? {#p3 1.120}

The sages told him that they saw, with their inner sight, that he would have children, sinless and blessed and like the gods. He should strive for what destiny was already pointing to. {#p4 1.120}

***

::scene{#s2 frame=pandu,kunti light=pandu,kunti}

Pandu remembered the curse and thought hard. Then he spoke to [Kunti](kunti) alone. A son, he said, was the source of a man's good name in the three worlds; without one, no sacrifice or gift or vow could save him. His own power to father children had been destroyed by the deer's curse. The law, he told her, recognised many kinds of sons, and in distress a husband could ask that his wife bear children by a worthy man. He asked her to do so, with a Brahmin of great austerity. {#p5 1.120}

Kunti would not hear of it. She was his wife, devoted to him, she said; she would not take any other man even in her thoughts. And she told him an old story, of a king named [Vyushitashva](i12433_vyushitashva) of Puru's line, who died young, and of his grieving widow Bhadra, to whom a voice from the sky promised children from her dead husband. Let Pandu, by his own great austerity, do the same. {#p6 1.121}

::aside{#a1 kind=note src=1.121,1.122}
**Two old stories, briefly.** Kunti's tale of Bhadra and Pandu's answer are both stories the epic's characters tell about the customs of an older age. We give them only in outline. They matter because they show what this couple believed was lawful, and how hard the choice was for Kunti.
::

***

::scene{#s3 frame=pandu,kunti,i11075_uddalaka,i2890_shvetaketu light=pandu}

Pandu answered with older stories still. In ancient times, he said, the rules of marriage had been different; the custom that a wife keeps to one husband for life was established by the sage [Shvetaketu](i2890_shvetaketu), the son of [Uddalaka](i11075_uddalaka), and he told her how and why. And he reminded her of how he himself, and his brothers, had been born through Vyasa, for the sake of the Kuru line. {#p7 1.122}

Then the king joined his palms and raised them to his head before his wife, and asked her once more to give him sons, so that he might go where fathers go. {#p8 1.122}

***

::scene{#s4 frame=kunti,durvasa,pandu light=kunti,durvasa}

So Kunti told him her secret, or part of it. In her girlhood in her father's house, she said, she had served the Brahmin Durvasa, and he had given her a mantra by which she could call any god she wished, who would come to her whether he liked it or not, and give her a child. {#p9 1.122}

> The words uttered by the Brahmana can never be false. The time also hath come when they may yield fruit. Commanded by thee, O royal sage, I can by that mantra summon any of the celestials, so that we may have good children. {#q2 1.122}
> — Kunti

::aside{#a2 kind=note src=1.122,1.111}
**What Kunti did not say.** She tells Pandu about the mantra. She does not tell him that she has used it once already, as a girl, or about the son she set adrift on the water.
::

***

::scene{#s5 frame=pandu,kunti,dharma light=dharma}

Pandu knew at once whom she should call. {#p10 1.122}

> Fortunate one, summon thou the god of justice. He is the most virtuous of the celestials. The god of justice and virtue will never be able to pollute us with sin. {#q3 1.122}
> — Pandu

The son of [Dharma](dharma), he said, would be the most virtuous of the Kurus, and would never set his heart on anything wrong. *So be it,* said Kunti. She bowed to her husband, walked around him in reverence, and made ready to do as he asked. {#p11 1.122}
